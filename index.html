  1. What's going on

    Seasons are short, and everyone around you wants the same result. When the team is losing, "whatever it takes" starts to sound reasonable.

    On the record

    One positive test for a banned performance drug takes you out for 365 days and costs a season of eligibility. A second one ends your college career. In 2025, Drury University had to give back a Division II swimming title after one athlete tested over the caffeine limit.

    NCAA drug-testing consent form, 2025-26; Swimming World, 2025.

    Your move

    Before you take anything new, run it through the tub check on this page. It takes about a minute.

  2. What's going on

    The people who decide your playing time are the same people you'd have to tell if you were struggling. That makes it hard to say anything at all.

    On the record

    NCAA rules say athletic staff who know an athlete is using a banned substance have to follow their school's procedures. So what you tell a coach or trainer may not stay with them.

    NCAA Bylaw 10.2, as printed in the Drug-Testing Manual.

    Your move

    Ask "what do you have to report?" before you share anything. Your campus counseling center is outside athletics and works under different privacy rules, so start there if you're unsure.

  3. What's going on

    Since July 2021, college athletes can get paid for their name, image and likeness. Your stats, your following and how you look in a jersey can turn into money now, which is one more reason to want results fast.

    On the record

    The FDA says SARMs are pushed at teens and young adults on social media as a quick way to gain muscle. They aren't approved for anyone to take, and the FDA has linked them to heart attacks, strokes and liver failure.

    FDA consumer warning on bodybuilding products.

    Your move

    If a brand sends you supplements as part of a deal, check them like any other tub. A free product can still cost you a season.

  4. What's going on

    If you think you have a real shot at the next level, every pound and every tenth of a second feels like it decides your future.

    On the record

    The NCAA says fewer than 2% of its athletes go on to play professionally. A positive test costs a full season, and that's a year scouts don't see you play.

    NCAA Recruiting Facts; NCAA drug-testing consent form, 2025-26.

    Your move

    Take the size or speed you want to your strength coach or a sports dietitian and ask for a plan with dates on it. Having a real timeline makes the shortcut less tempting.

  5. What's going on

    Some sports reward size and some reward being lean. Either one can leave you feeling like your body is wrong for your position. Plenty of people take steroids, SARMs or stimulants for how they look as much as for how they play.

    On the record

    Steroids can change your body in ways that don't go back: breast growth and hair loss in men, a deeper voice and body hair in women. In teenagers they can stop growth early.

    National Institute on Drug Abuse.

    Your move

    If thinking about food or your body takes up a lot of your day, tell a counselor. That's a normal thing to bring them, and they hear it from athletes a lot.

  6. What's going on

    It can feel like you're the only one playing clean, especially when a coach praises whoever got bigger over the summer and nobody asks how.

    On the record

    In the NCAA's 2023 anonymous survey of 23,272 athletes, 99.2% said they hadn't used any of the listed performance enhancers in the past year. People underreport on surveys, so the real figure is lower than that. It still isn't close to "everyone".

    NCAA Student-Athlete Health and Wellness Study, 2024.

    Your move

    You're allowed to say "I'll pass." If a coach or staff member is the one pushing it, tell someone outside the team, like a campus counselor, your compliance office or a parent.

  7. What's going on

    Lifts at 6 a.m., class, practice, travel, and not much sleep. When your body can't keep up, anything that promises faster recovery looks good.

    On the record

    Between 2017 and 2023, the share of NCAA athletes using creatine went from 13% to 23%, and energy drinks from 31% to 44%. Creatine isn't banned. The NCAA's warning is about the tub: supplements aren't well regulated, and athletes have lost eligibility over what was in them.

    NCAA Health and Wellness Study, 2024; NCAA Drug-Testing Manual.

    Your move

    Tell your athletic trainer you're run down. Being tired isn't a banned substance, so there's nothing for them to report. Then check what's already in your locker.

The numbers

We started this project thinking steroid use was climbing. The NCAA's own survey doesn't show that. In 2023, 99.2% of athletes said they hadn't touched any of the performance enhancers on its list.

What did climb is the legal stuff. Almost half of college athletes drink energy drinks now, and creatine use nearly doubled in six years. None of that is against the rules. The trouble is that nobody checks a supplement before it goes on the shelf, and the athlete takes the penalty when a tub has something banned in it.

These are self-reported answers on a paper survey handed out by a faculty rep, so read them as the low end.

What these drugs are

Five groups cover most of what comes up in a college weight room. Here's what people hope each one does, and what it can do instead.

Name The hope What it can cost you NCAA status
Anabolic steroids Lab-made versions of testosterone More muscle and strength, quickly. Acne, high blood pressure, and heart and liver damage. In men, shrunken testicles and breast growth. In women, a deeper voice and body hair. Some of it is permanent. Banned

Tested year-round in Divisions I and II.
SARMs Ostarine, LGD-4033 and similar The gains of a steroid without the side effects. They aren't approved for human use at all. The FDA has reports of heart attack, stroke and liver failure, and they're often sold labeled as supplements or "research chemicals". Banned

Counted as anabolic agents.
HGH and peptides Human growth hormone Faster recovery, leaner build. Swelling, joint pain, carpal tunnel and blood sugar problems. Studies haven't found much of a strength gain to go with them. Banned

The rules in plain English

This is how NCAA testing works as of the 2025-26 materials. Rules change, and your compliance office has the current version.

  • You can be tested any time

    A company called Drug Free Sport runs testing for the NCAA. Division I and II athletes can be picked year-round, and anyone can be tested at a championship.

  • One positive costs a year

    For a performance drug, you sit out 365 days and lose a season of eligibility in every sport you play. You also have to test clean before you come back.

  • A second positive ends it

    You lose all the eligibility you have left, regular season and postseason.

  • Skipping a test counts as failing

    Not showing up or refusing to give a sample gets the same penalty as a positive result.

  • Prescriptions need paperwork first

    If a doctor prescribes something on the banned list, your school has to file a medical exception. For hormones and anabolic agents, the NCAA has to approve it before you practice or compete.

  • Some schools have a Safe Harbor

    That's a policy that lets you come forward before a test and get treatment without school penalties. It often works only once, and an NCAA test can still catch you while you're in it. Your student-athlete handbook will say if your school has one.

Sources: NCAA Drug-Testing Manual; NCAA Division I drug-testing consent form, 2025-26; Cleveland State University safe harbor policy.

Check the tub

The NCAA says no supplement is ever risk-free, even a certified one. These four steps lower the risk a lot.

  1. Ask if you need it

    A sports dietitian can tell you whether food already covers it. Most of the time it does.

  2. Look for a third-party seal

    NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport both test products for banned substances. Look the lot number up on the certifier's website. A logo on the tub isn't proof.

  3. Ask AXIS

    Drug Free Sport AXIS reviews labels for NCAA athletes. Call 816-474-7321 or go to axis.drugfreesport.com. Your athletic trainer or compliance office can give you the access code.

  4. Keep the container

    Hang on to the tub and the receipt until it's finished. If a test ever comes back wrong, that's your evidence.

What we want to change

Testing catches people after the fact. We think four groups could do something earlier, and none of it needs a new rule.

Who What we're asking Why it's fair to ask
Athletes Check every supplement before you take it. Tell one person outside your team when the pressure gets heavy, and do it before you've started using anything. The NCAA puts the penalty on the athlete even when the label was wrong.
Coaches Say out loud, and more than once, that asking for help won't cost anyone their spot. When someone gets a lot bigger fast, ask how before you praise it. A 2019 review of 52 studies found stigma is the most common reason athletes don't get mental health help, and that coaches set the tone for it.
Athletic departments Write a Safe Harbor policy if you don't have one, and remind athletes about it every season. Run peer-led sessions during recruiting visits and freshman year. Some schools already do this. In a trial with 31 high school football teams, sessions run by coaches and student team leaders lowered players' intent to use steroids.
Universities Pay for confidential counseling that sits outside athletics, and keep an anonymous resource like this one online. NCAA rules make athletic staff act on what they learn about banned substances, so athletes need somewhere else to go first.

Sources: Castaldelli-Maia et al., 2019; Goldberg et al., 1996 and 2000; NCAA Bylaw 10.2, as printed in the Drug-Testing Manual.

A 30-minute team session

This is the session plan from our project. A current or former athlete runs it, and the coaches stay out of the room. The peer-led part comes from ATLAS, a high school program where student team leaders ran sessions with their coaches.

  1. 0:00Guess the number

    Everyone writes down what share of college athletes they think used a performance enhancer last year. Then put the survey answer on the board, which is under 1%. If the guesses land way above it, talk about why.

  2. 0:05The lineup

    In small groups, pick the pressure from the roster that fits your sport and talk about what it sounds like on your team. Nobody has to talk about themselves.

  3. 0:15Read the tub

    Pass around two real supplement containers and keep score on each label with the flag card.

  4. 0:25Who would you tell

    Each person writes down one name outside the team and saves two phone numbers from this page.

Talk to a person

Nobody reads messages on this site, because there's nowhere on it to send one. These people do pick up.

  • 988

    Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Call or text 988, any hour. You don't need to be suicidal to use it.

  • 1-800-662-4357

    SAMHSA National Helpline. Free, open 24 hours, in English and Spanish. They don't counsel you on the call. They point you to treatment and support near you.

  • 911

    For an emergency. Chest pain, trouble breathing, or someone who has passed out. Say what was taken if you know.

  • Campus counseling

    Most universities have a counseling center, and it's separate from athletics. Ask what stays private before you start.

  • A doctor outside the team

    Your own doctor or the student health center can run blood work and tell you where you stand.

  • Your athletic trainer

    They know sports medicine better than anyone on this list, and a lot of them will go to bat for you. Ask first: "What do you have to report?"
